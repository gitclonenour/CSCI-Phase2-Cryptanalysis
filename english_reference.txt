People communicate through words because language allows them to share information, describe events, ask questions, and exchange ideas. A person who reads an interesting article may want to discuss its meaning with friends or family. Clear writing is useful when people work together, and careful listening is important when they disagree. A good explanation introduces the subject, provides enough context, and then gives examples that a reader can understand. Different situations call for different kinds of language. A short message can be direct, while a report may require detailed evidence and a conclusion.

A secure communication system should protect messages from unauthorized readers. Encryption changes readable information into a different representation, and decryption reverses that transformation when the correct secret information is available. Classical substitution systems replace letters according to fixed rules. Although they are interesting to study, they often reveal statistical patterns that make them vulnerable to analysis. The most common letters and combinations of letters can help an attacker form guesses about hidden text. Strong modern systems are designed to resist such simple patterns.

An effective dictionary helps readers discover the meanings of unfamiliar words. When analyzing an encrypted message, however, a dictionary is only one source of evidence. A sequence of letters can occasionally form a valid word by chance. Longer words and repeated language patterns usually provide stronger evidence than isolated short words. A program that proposes possible messages should therefore report uncertainty rather than claim that its first guess must be correct.
